# Assessor reasoning: the hidden copy

Read this after making your own attempt in [the worksheet](README.md). A good answer names the customer promise and the evidence needed, rather than choosing the smallest number on the screen.

The no-expiry scenario retains the 4 MiB original for 30 modeled days and the 6 MiB replacement for 20. The worksheet prints **240 MiB-days** of retained data. Removing the original on day 20 changes that to **200 MiB-days**; the replacement still remains through day 30. The ordinary day-20 delete marker does not itself remove either data version. The model's CU storage line changes, while its assumed 40 earlier GETs and transfer volume stay the same. These counts follow from the fictional inputs, not an S3 usage report.

If the customer requests the prior version after the marker, first decide whether the business promise and retention policy permit that read. An authorized version-specific request can retrieve a retained prior version; a normal GET sees the marker and behaves as if the object is deleted. Add any successful GET, the actual returned bytes and the chosen network path to the meter inventory. Check the identity's S3 version-read permission and, for SSE-KMS, the applicable KMS decrypt permission. A version ID is an address, not a permission grant. [AWS version deletion](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/DeletingObjectVersions.html), [SSE-KMS permissions](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/UsingKMSEncryption.html).

If the promise changes to “every copy gone within 24 hours,” stop before selecting a cost winner. Define which versions, replicas, backups, exports and legal holds count. Design a permitted permanent-removal path, then gather version-aware inventory and deletion/readback evidence. A configured lifecycle rule is a request for future action, not proof of completion at an exact hour. The retention decision may make the new promise impossible or unlawful; resolve that conflict with the owner before changing a rule. [AWS lifecycle expiration](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/lifecycle-expire-general-considerations.html).

Finally, the CU model leaves KMS calls unknown because GET count alone does not determine them, especially when S3 Bucket Keys and requester patterns vary. It also leaves public regional prices, selected storage class terms, retries, logs and other services outside its subtotal. The separate public-price extension covers four dated meters but still omits those other costs and account facts. The pass condition is an explicit **hold** on deployment until a current, complete whole-run estimate within the account owner's chosen limit, an appropriate credit reserve, scoped non-root identity, cleanup plan and selected-account facts are reviewed. Neither subtotal can satisfy that condition.

The dated public-price extension narrows one earlier omission. Under its selected Ohio S3 Standard and internet-out rates, both retained versions produce a USD 0.0212994375 gross subtotal; authorized old-version removal on day 20 produces about USD 0.0212694896. Only storage differs. GET and transfer stay constant because all forty downloads happened before the marker. The transfer component is USD 0.02109375 in each path, far larger than the small storage delta. A reader who concludes “the cleanup does not matter” has confused price with the customer's retention or deletion promise. A reader who calls either subtotal “the AWS bill” has ignored the shared transfer allowance, credits, other meters and account facts. [S3 pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/s3/pricing/).

The emulated recovery bridge has a different evidence mix. Moto's version inventory supplies the three data sizes; the 30-day retention window, three PUTs, two GETs and absent internet delivery are assumptions. The ordinary delete marker does not reduce the two original data-version sizes, and staging a candidate adds a third data version. Removing the confirmed old version on modeled day 20 lowers retained byte-days, but the old version ID can no longer be used for that recovery; the staged candidate is a separate copy with its own authority and retention decision. Its selected subtotal omits actual version-list and marker charges, KMS, retries, customer transfer, account allowances and credits. The correct decision is to keep the deployment gate closed and revise the whole-run meter list for the real workload. An AI answer that labels Moto sizes as an AWS bill, or treats the smaller subtotal as permission to delete the old version, fails this case.
